## Why Internal Temperature Is the Only Reliable Doneness Test

Cooking time calculators, visual cues, and touch tests all have their place, but internal temperature is the only method that's both precise and safe. A 3-pound chicken can take anywhere from 60 to 90 minutes depending on your oven's accuracy, the bird's starting temperature, whether it was brined, and a dozen other variables. An instant-read thermometer eliminates all that guesswork in two seconds.

This chart covers every common protein with both USDA-recommended minimum safe temperatures and the practical temperatures used by chefs for optimal texture and flavor. These two numbers often differ — the USDA recommends cooking all pork to 145°F, but many chefs pull a pork tenderloin at 140°F knowing that carryover cooking will bring it to 145°F during rest. We've included both so you can make an informed decision.

## Beef Internal Temperatures

Beef offers the widest range of doneness options. The USDA recommends a minimum of 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest for whole cuts (steaks, roasts). Ground beef requires 160°F (71°C) with no rest time needed.

### Steaks & Roasts

| Doneness    | Pull Temp (°F) | Pull Temp (°C) | After Rest (°F) | Description                        |
|-------------|:--------------:|:--------------:|:----------------:|------------------------------------|
| Rare        | 120°F          | 49°C           | 125°F            | Cool red center, very soft         |
| Medium-Rare | 130°F          | 54°C           | 135°F            | Warm red center, firm outer ring   |
| Medium      | 140°F          | 60°C           | 145°F            | Warm pink center throughout        |
| Medium-Well | 150°F          | 66°C           | 155°F            | Slight pink, mostly gray-brown     |
| Well-Done   | 160°F          | 71°C           | 165°F            | No pink, uniformly brown           |

**USDA Minimum:** 145°F (63°C) with 3-minute rest = equivalent to medium doneness.

**Rest Time:** 5–10 minutes for steaks, 15–20 minutes for roasts. Tent loosely with foil — tight wrapping traps steam and softens the crust.

### Prime Rib / Standing Rib Roast

Prime rib follows the same temperatures as above but requires extra attention to carryover cooking. A large standing rib roast can rise 10–15°F after leaving the oven (compared to 5°F for a steak), so pull it earlier than you think.

For a medium-rare prime rib, pull at **120–125°F** and rest for 20–30 minutes. It will coast up to 135°F.

→ **Need exact cooking times by weight?** Use our [Meat Cooking Time Calculator](/meat-cooking-time-calculator) — enter your roast weight and it'll calculate time at the right oven temperature.

### Brisket

Brisket is the exception to every temperature rule. You cook it to 195–205°F (90–96°C) — well past the "well-done" range — because the goal is to break down the tough collagen into gelatin. This only happens above 180°F, and the ideal probe-tender texture arrives around 200–203°F. The brisket is done when a thermometer probe slides in with no resistance, like poking warm butter.

## Pork Internal Temperatures

The USDA updated its pork guidelines in 2011, lowering the recommended temperature from 160°F to **145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest** for whole cuts. This was a significant change — it means properly cooked pork can and should be slightly pink in the center.

### Pork Chops, Tenderloin & Loin Roasts

| Doneness    | Pull Temp (°F) | Pull Temp (°C) | After Rest (°F) | Description                   |
|-------------|:--------------:|:--------------:|:----------------:|-------------------------------|
| Medium      | 140°F          | 60°C           | 145°F            | Slight pink, juicy            |
| Medium-Well | 150°F          | 66°C           | 155°F            | Barely pink                   |
| Well-Done   | 160°F          | 71°C           | 165°F            | No pink, can be dry           |

**Rest Time:** 5–10 minutes for chops, 10–15 minutes for roasts.

Most chefs target 145°F as the sweet spot for pork — juicy with a faint pink center. Overcooking pork to 160°F+ is the single most common reason for dry, tough pork chops.

### Pulled Pork (Pork Shoulder / Boston Butt)

Like brisket, pulled pork is cooked to **195–205°F** (90–96°C) to break down connective tissue. The collagen-to-gelatin conversion happens between 160°F and 190°F (the "stall" where the temperature plateaus for hours). Once it reaches 200–203°F and shreds easily with a fork, it's done.

→ **Calculate cook time for your pork shoulder:** Our [Meat Cooking Time Calculator](/meat-cooking-time-calculator) estimates smoking and oven times based on weight.

### Pork Ribs

Back ribs: **190–200°F** internal. Spare ribs: **195–205°F**. The "bend test" is a visual backup — pick up the rack with tongs at the center. If the meat cracks on the surface and the ribs bend sharply, they're ready.

## Poultry Internal Temperatures

All poultry (chicken, turkey, duck, game hen) must reach a minimum of **165°F (74°C)** per USDA guidelines. Unlike beef and pork, there is no rare or medium option for poultry — undercooked chicken is a serious salmonella and campylobacter risk.

### Chicken

| Cut             | Minimum Temp (°F) | Minimum Temp (°C) | Notes                                 |
|-----------------|:------------------:|:------------------:|---------------------------------------|
| Whole chicken   | 165°F              | 74°C               | Measure in thickest part of thigh     |
| Breast          | 165°F              | 74°C               | Pull at 160°F, rest to 165°F          |
| Thighs/Legs     | 175–180°F          | 79–82°C            | Higher temp = better texture (more collagen) |
| Wings           | 165°F              | 74°C               | Difficult to probe — cook until juices run clear |
| Ground chicken  | 165°F              | 74°C               | No rest needed                        |

**The thigh vs. breast dilemma:** Dark meat (thighs, legs) benefits from cooking to 175–180°F because the extra collagen breaks down and the meat becomes more tender and flavorful. White meat (breast) dries out rapidly past 165°F. This is why spatchcocking (butterflying) a whole chicken is the best technique — it lets the thighs cook hotter and faster while the breast stays protected.

**Rest Time:** 10–15 minutes for a whole bird, 5 minutes for individual breasts. Internal temperature will rise about 5°F during rest.

### Turkey

| Cut             | Minimum Temp (°F) | Minimum Temp (°C) | Notes                                 |
|-----------------|:------------------:|:------------------:|---------------------------------------|
| Whole turkey    | 165°F              | 74°C               | Measure in inner thigh, away from bone |
| Turkey breast   | 165°F              | 74°C               | Pull at 160°F, rest to 165°F          |
| Stuffed turkey  | 165°F              | 74°C               | Measure stuffing center AND thigh     |
| Ground turkey   | 165°F              | 74°C               | No rest needed                        |

**Critical for Thanksgiving:** Always measure temperature in the inner thigh (the thickest part, between the leg and body, not touching bone). If you're cooking a stuffed turkey, the center of the stuffing must also reach 165°F — this is why many food safety experts recommend cooking stuffing separately.

→ **Planning a turkey dinner?** Our [Turkey Cooking Time Calculator](/turkey-cooking-calculator) gives you exact times based on bird weight, stuffed vs. unstuffed, and cooking method (roast, smoke, deep-fry).

### Duck

Duck breast is the one poultry exception many chefs treat differently. Because duck breast has a thick fat cap and a different muscle structure than chicken, many chefs serve it medium-rare to medium (130–140°F / 54–60°C). The USDA still recommends 165°F for all poultry. If you choose to cook duck breast below 165°F, understand that you're accepting some food safety risk.

Whole roasted duck: **165°F** minimum in the thigh, same as chicken.

## Lamb Internal Temperatures

Lamb follows the same USDA guideline as beef: **145°F (63°C) minimum with a 3-minute rest** for whole cuts. Lamb's flavor is robust enough to stand up to rare and medium-rare cooking, and many lamb-lovers prefer it pink.

### Lamb Chops, Leg & Rack of Lamb

| Doneness    | Pull Temp (°F) | Pull Temp (°C) | After Rest (°F) | Description                   |
|-------------|:--------------:|:--------------:|:----------------:|-------------------------------|
| Rare        | 120°F          | 49°C           | 125°F            | Cool red center               |
| Medium-Rare | 130°F          | 54°C           | 135°F            | Warm red center (most popular)|
| Medium      | 140°F          | 60°C           | 145°F            | Warm pink throughout          |
| Medium-Well | 150°F          | 66°C           | 155°F            | Slight pink                   |
| Well-Done   | 160°F          | 71°C           | 165°F            | No pink                       |

**Rest Time:** 5–10 minutes for chops, 15–20 minutes for leg or rack.

### Lamb Shoulder / Leg (Slow-Roasted)

When slow-roasting lamb for pulled/shredded texture, cook to **190–200°F** — same principle as pulled pork and brisket. The collagen breakdown gives you fall-apart tenderness.

## Fish & Seafood Internal Temperatures

Fish has a lower safe temperature than meat because the pathogens found in fish are killed at lower temperatures. The USDA recommends **145°F (63°C)** for fin fish.

| Seafood       | USDA Minimum (°F) | Preferred (°F)      | Notes                              |
|---------------|:------------------:|:-------------------:|------------------------------------|
| Salmon        | 145°F              | 120–130°F (medium)  | Many chefs prefer medium; translucent center |
| Tuna (steak)  | 145°F              | 115–120°F (rare)    | Often served rare/seared           |
| White fish (cod, halibut, tilapia) | 145°F | 135–140°F | Flakes easily with fork            |
| Shrimp        | 145°F              | Cook until pink/opaque | No thermometer needed — visual cue |
| Scallops      | 145°F              | Sear 2 min/side     | Golden outside, translucent center |
| Lobster tail  | 145°F              | 140°F               | Opaque throughout, slightly firm   |
| Crab          | 145°F              | Until shell turns red | Already cooked if purchased frozen |

**Rest Time:** Fish doesn't need resting the way red meat does. Serve immediately — carryover cooking is minimal (1–2°F).

**Sushi-grade fish:** Serving raw fish is outside the scope of these USDA cooking guidelines. Sushi-grade fish should be previously frozen to kill parasites (FDA recommends -4°F for 7 days or -31°F for 15 hours).

## Ground Meat Temperatures

Ground meat must be cooked to higher temperatures than whole cuts. When meat is ground, surface bacteria get mixed throughout, so the interior needs to reach a temperature that kills pathogens.

| Ground Meat    | USDA Minimum (°F) | USDA Minimum (°C) | Notes                        |
|----------------|:------------------:|:------------------:|------------------------------|
| Ground beef    | 160°F              | 71°C               | No rest needed               |
| Ground pork    | 160°F              | 71°C               | No rest needed               |
| Ground lamb    | 160°F              | 71°C               | No rest needed               |
| Ground chicken | 165°F              | 74°C               | No rest needed               |
| Ground turkey  | 165°F              | 74°C               | No rest needed               |

**Why 160°F with no rest for ground beef but 145°F with rest for steaks?** When you grind meat, bacteria from the surface gets distributed throughout. A whole steak only has bacteria on the outside, which is killed instantly by searing. Ground meat has potential bacteria throughout, requiring a higher minimum temperature to be safe.

This is why a medium-rare burger (130–135°F) carries more risk than a medium-rare steak at the same temperature. If you want a pink burger, grinding the meat yourself from a whole, seared cut can reduce (but not eliminate) the risk.

## Understanding Carryover Cooking

Carryover cooking is the continued rise in internal temperature after you remove meat from the heat source. It happens because the exterior of the meat is much hotter than the interior, and heat continues to transfer inward after cooking stops.

How much temperature rise to expect:

| Cut / Size                  | Expected Rise |
|-----------------------------|:-------------:|
| Steaks (1–1.5" thick)      | 3–5°F         |
| Thick-cut chops            | 5–8°F         |
| Pork loin roast            | 5–10°F        |
| Whole chicken              | 5–10°F        |
| Prime rib / large roast    | 10–15°F       |
| Whole turkey               | 10–15°F       |
| Brisket / pork shoulder    | 5°F (minimal) |
| Fish fillets               | 1–3°F         |

**The rule:** The larger and thicker the cut, the more carryover cooking you'll get. Pull your meat at a temperature below your target, and let the carryover do the rest. This is why every temperature chart above includes both "pull temp" and "after rest" numbers where applicable.

## Where to Insert the Thermometer

Correct thermometer placement makes the difference between an accurate reading and a misleading one.

**General rule:** Insert into the geometric center of the thickest part, avoiding bone, fat, and gristle. Bone conducts heat faster than meat, so a reading near bone will be falsely high. Fat has different thermal properties and will give an inaccurate reading.

**Whole chicken or turkey:** Insert into the inner thigh — the area between the drumstick and the body. Angle the probe toward the body without touching the thighbone.

**Steaks:** Insert from the side, horizontally, into the center. For thin steaks (under 1 inch), angle the probe through the side at about 45 degrees so the sensing tip reaches the middle.

**Roasts:** Insert at the center of the thickest section. For irregularly shaped roasts, check multiple spots.

**Burgers:** Insert from the side, into the center. Difficult with thin patties — a leave-in probe thermometer works better than an instant-read for burgers.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Medium-Rare Ribeye Steak

You want a medium-rare ribeye. Target after rest: 135°F. Expected carryover for a 1.5-inch steak: about 5°F. Pull the steak at **130°F** and rest for 5 minutes. Use our [Meat Cooking Time Calculator](/meat-cooking-time-calculator) to estimate cooking time based on thickness.

### Example 2: Thanksgiving Turkey

Your 15-pound turkey needs to reach 165°F in the thigh. Carryover for a whole turkey: 10–15°F. Pull when the thigh reads **155–160°F**, tent with foil, and rest 20–30 minutes. The thigh will coast to 165–170°F. Check the breast too — pull earlier if the breast hits 160°F. Our [Turkey Cooking Time Calculator](/turkey-cooking-calculator) will estimate total roasting time.

### Example 3: Slow-Smoked Pork Shoulder

You're smoking an 8-pound pork shoulder for pulled pork. The temperature will stall around 150–170°F for several hours (the evaporative cooling "stall"). Push through it or wrap in foil/butcher paper (the "Texas crutch"). Target: **203°F**, probe-tender. Expected cook time: 1–1.5 hours per pound at 225°F. Use our [Meat Cooking Time Calculator](/meat-cooking-time-calculator) for a more precise estimate.

### Example 4: Pan-Seared Salmon

You prefer medium salmon with a translucent center. USDA says 145°F, but you're targeting 125°F for texture. Sear skin-side down for 4 minutes, flip, cook 2 more minutes. Insert thermometer from the side at the thickest point. Carryover is minimal for fish (1–2°F), so pull right at your target temperature.

### Example 5: Rack of Lamb for a Dinner Party

You're serving rack of lamb medium-rare for guests. Target after rest: 135°F. Carryover for a rack: about 5–8°F. Sear the rack, roast at 400°F, and pull at **128–130°F**. Rest 10 minutes. Slice between the bones to serve.

### Example 6: Safe Burgers for a Family BBQ

You're grilling burgers for the family and want them safe without being hockey pucks. USDA minimum for ground beef: 160°F. Use an instant-read thermometer inserted from the side. For juicier results at 160°F, use ground beef with 20% fat content (80/20) — the extra fat keeps the burger moist even at well-done temperatures.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What temperature kills bacteria in meat?
Most foodborne pathogens (salmonella, E. coli, listeria) are killed at 165°F (74°C) instantly. At lower temperatures like 145°F (63°C), bacteria are killed over time — which is why the USDA recommends a 3-minute rest at 145°F for whole cuts. The combination of temperature and time achieves the same level of food safety.

### Is pink pork safe to eat?
Yes. Since 2011, the USDA has recommended cooking whole pork cuts to 145°F with a 3-minute rest. At this temperature, pork will have a slightly pink center, which is perfectly safe. The old recommendation of 160°F was based on concerns about trichinosis, which has been effectively eliminated in commercially raised pigs.

### Can I eat steak rare?
Whole-muscle steaks are generally safe at lower temperatures because bacteria exist primarily on the surface, which is killed by searing. The USDA recommends 145°F minimum, but many restaurants serve steaks rare (125°F) and medium-rare (135°F). People with compromised immune systems, pregnant women, young children, and the elderly should follow USDA minimums.

### What's the USDA minimum for chicken?
All chicken (whole, breasts, thighs, ground) must reach 165°F (74°C). There is no safe lower temperature for poultry under standard home cooking methods. If using sous vide, lower temperatures can be safe when held for extended periods, but that's a specialized technique requiring precise equipment.

### Do I really need a meat thermometer?
Yes. The "poke test" and "juices run clear" methods are unreliable. Studies show that visual assessment correctly identifies doneness less than 50% of the time. A basic instant-read thermometer costs about $15 and takes 2–3 seconds to give you a definitive answer. It's the single most impactful tool upgrade for any home cook.

### What does "let the meat rest" actually do?
During cooking, heat drives moisture toward the center. If you cut immediately, those concentrated juices run out onto the cutting board. Resting allows the temperature to equalize, the muscle fibers to relax, and the juices to redistribute throughout the meat. The result is more juice in every bite and less on your plate.

### How accurate are oven-safe leave-in thermometers?
Most analog (dial) leave-in thermometers are accurate to within ±2–3°F. Digital probe thermometers are accurate to ±1°F and respond faster. For the best results, use a digital instant-read thermometer for spot-checking and a digital probe with a cable or wireless transmitter for monitoring roasts and whole birds in the oven.
