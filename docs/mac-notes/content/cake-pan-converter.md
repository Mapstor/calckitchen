# Cake Pan Converter — Resize Any Baking Recipe for a Different Pan

Ever found the perfect cake recipe only to realize it's written for a 9-inch (23 cm) round pan and you only have an 8-inch (20 cm) square? Or maybe you need to scale a recipe from two 8-inch (20 cm) layers to a single 10-inch (25 cm) pan for a different presentation. Our **Cake Pan Converter** calculates exact ingredient multipliers when switching between any pan sizes and shapes — round, square, rectangular, Bundt, springform, and loaf.

The key insight most bakers miss: when you change pan size, you can't just eyeball the batter. A 10-inch (25 cm) round pan holds **56% more** batter than an 8-inch (20 cm) round. If you pour an 8-inch recipe into a 10-inch pan without adjusting, you'll get a flat, thin cake. If you pour a 10-inch recipe into an 8-inch pan, you'll get overflow and a smoky oven.

---

## Why Pan Volume Matters (Not Just Diameter)

The most common mistake in pan conversion is thinking linearly. If you're going from a 9-inch pan to a 12-inch pan, you might think you need about 33% more batter (because 12 is 33% more than 9). But pans are two-dimensional surfaces — area scales with the **square** of the dimension.

A 12-inch round pan has an area of ~113 square inches, while a 9-inch round pan has ~63.6 square inches. That's **78% more area**, not 33%. You'd need nearly double the recipe, not just a third more.

This is why a proper pan conversion calculator is essential. The math isn't intuitive, and getting it wrong ruins bakes.

### The Conversion Formula

The core formula for pan conversion is:

**Ingredient Multiplier = New Pan Volume ÷ Original Pan Volume**

For pans of the same depth, this simplifies to:

**Ingredient Multiplier = New Pan Area ÷ Original Pan Area**

If your multiplier is 1.5, you multiply every ingredient by 1.5. If it's 0.75, you use 75% of each ingredient.

## How to Calculate Volume Adjustments and Ingredient Multipliers

### Step-by-Step Method

**Step 1:** Find the volume (or area, if pans are the same depth) of your original pan and your new pan from the tables above.

**Step 2:** Calculate the multiplier: New Pan Volume ÷ Original Pan Volume.

**Step 3:** Multiply every ingredient by that number.

**Step 4:** Handle eggs with smart rounding (see below).

### Worked Example: Converting a 9-inch (23 cm) round to a 6-inch (15 cm) round

Original recipe (for one 9" / 23 cm round layer):
- 1½ cups flour
- 1 cup sugar
- 2 eggs
- ½ cup butter
- ¾ cup milk

**Multiplier:** 6" (15 cm) area ÷ 9" (23 cm) area = 28.3 ÷ 63.6 = **0.445**

Converted recipe:
- Flour: 1.5 cups × 0.445 = 0.67 cups → **⅔ cup**
- Sugar: 1 cup × 0.445 = **scant ½ cup** (≈ 7 tbsp)
- Eggs: 2 × 0.445 = 0.89 → **1 egg**
- Butter: 0.5 cup × 0.445 = 0.22 cups → **3½ tablespoons**
- Milk: 0.75 cup × 0.445 = 0.33 cups → **⅓ cup**

### Handling Fractional Eggs

Eggs are the trickiest ingredient in pan conversion because you can't easily use 0.445 of an egg. Here's the system professional bakers use:

| Multiplied Egg Value | What to Use |
|---|---|
| 0.25–0.75 | 1 egg |
| 0.76–1.25 | 1 egg |
| 1.26–1.75 | 1 egg + 1 yolk OR 2 eggs |
| 1.76–2.25 | 2 eggs |
| 2.26–2.75 | 2 eggs + 1 yolk OR 3 eggs |

For precision, you can also beat an egg and measure by volume: one large egg is approximately 3 tablespoons (about 50ml). So 0.5 of an egg ≈ 1.5 tablespoons of beaten egg.

## 6 Real-Life Examples

### Example 1: Recipe calls for 9" (23 cm) round, you have 8" (20 cm) round

**Multiplier:** 50.3 ÷ 63.6 = **0.79**

Use about 80% of every ingredient. If the recipe calls for 2 cups flour, use 1⅗ cups (round to 1½ cups + 2 tablespoons). For 3 eggs, use 2 eggs + 1 yolk. The cake will be the same height in the smaller pan — no adjustment to baking time needed, though check 2–3 minutes early.

### Example 2: Making a 2-layer 10" (25 cm) cake from an 8" (20 cm) recipe

Your recipe makes one 8" (20 cm) layer. You need two 10" (25 cm) layers.

**Multiplier for one 10" (25 cm) layer:** 78.5 ÷ 50.3 = 1.56
**Multiplier for two 10" (25 cm) layers:** 1.56 × 2 = **3.12**

Triple the entire recipe and add about 10% more. For a recipe with 1.5 cups flour per layer, you'd need about 4.7 cups total — round to 4¾ cups. **Important:** When scaling baking recipes this much (3×+), chemical leaveners (baking powder, baking soda) don't scale linearly. Use about 2.5× the leavening instead of 3× to avoid a metallic taste or collapsed structure.

### Example 3: Converting a 9" (23 cm) round recipe to a 10-cup Bundt

**Multiplier:** 10 cups ÷ 8.0 cups (9" / 23 cm round volume) = **1.25**

Increase everything by 25%. A Bundt pan is deeper and has a center tube, so the cake bakes differently — add 10–15 minutes to the baking time and reduce oven temperature by 25°F (15°C). Test with a skewer in the thickest part.

### Example 4: Two 8" (20 cm) round layers → one 9" × 13" (23 × 33 cm) sheet

Combined volume of two 8" (20 cm) rounds: 6.3 cups × 2 = 12.6 cups. Volume of 9" × 13" (23 × 33 cm) sheet: 14.6 cups.

**Multiplier from doubled recipe:** 14.6 ÷ 12.6 = **1.16**

The sheet pan is slightly larger, but most bakers just pour the doubled recipe into the 9" × 13" and accept a slightly thinner cake. The difference is small enough that the cake still works perfectly. Reduce baking time by 5–10 minutes since the batter is spread thinner.

### Example 5: Scaling a 12-cup Bundt recipe for mini Bundts

Mini Bundt pans hold 1 cup each. **Multiplier per mini Bundt:** 1 ÷ 12 = **0.083** (but you don't calculate per mini — you just make the full recipe and fill 12 mini Bundts). The full recipe fills exactly 12 standard mini Bundts. Bake at the same temperature but reduce time to 15–20 minutes (roughly half the time of a full Bundt).

### Example 6: Converting a 9" (23 cm) round to an 8" (20 cm) square for a different look

**Multiplier:** 64 (8" / 20 cm square area) ÷ 63.6 (9" / 23 cm round area) = **1.006**

These two pans are virtually identical in volume. Use the exact same recipe — no adjustment needed. This is the single most useful pan substitution to memorize.

## Baking Time Adjustments When Changing Pans

Changing pan size affects baking time because it changes the thickness of the batter. Here are general guidelines:

| Change | Baking Time Adjustment |
|---|---|
| Larger pan (thinner batter) | Reduce time by 5–10 minutes |
| Smaller pan (thicker batter) | Increase time by 5–10 minutes |
| Bundt pan (deep, center tube) | Add 10–15 minutes, reduce temp 25°F (15°C) |
| Sheet pan (thin, wide) | Reduce time by 10–15 minutes |
| Mini pans (cupcakes, mini Bundt) | Reduce time by 40–50% |

Always use the toothpick test: insert a toothpick into the center of the cake. If it comes out clean or with just a few moist crumbs, the cake is done. Start checking 5 minutes before your estimated time.

## Pro Tips for Pan Conversion

**Measure your pan's actual volume.** Pan dimensions listed on packaging aren't always accurate, and older pans may be non-standard sizes. To find the exact volume, fill your pan with water using a measuring cup and count the cups.

**Fill pans ⅔ to ¾ full.** This is the universal rule for cake pans. If your converted recipe yields more batter than ¾ of the pan's capacity, you have too much — bake the excess as cupcakes rather than risking overflow.

**Dark pans bake faster.** If you're switching from a light-colored pan to a dark or non-stick pan (or vice versa), adjust temperature by 25°F (15°C). Dark pans absorb more heat, so reduce the temperature by 25°F (15°C) to prevent over-browning.

**Leavening doesn't scale linearly.** When scaling up by more than 2×, use slightly less baking powder/soda than the math suggests (about 80–90% of the calculated amount). Too much leavener creates a risen cake that then collapses.

**Grease and prepare the new pan properly.** Different pan shapes need different prep. Bundt pans need thorough greasing in every crevice (use a pastry brush). Springform pans benefit from parchment on the bottom. Sheet pans should be lined with parchment with a slight overhang for easy removal.

**When in doubt, round down.** If your multiplier gives you an awkward number, it's better to have slightly less batter (which just means a marginally shorter cake) than too much batter (which means overflow and smoke).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I use an 8-inch (20 cm) square pan instead of a 9-inch (23 cm) round?

Yes — this is the most common pan substitution and it works perfectly. An 8-inch (20 cm) square pan has 64 square inches (413 sq cm) of area, and a 9-inch (23 cm) round has 63.6 square inches (410 sq cm). They're virtually identical, so use the same recipe without adjustments.

### How do I convert a recipe from round to rectangular?

Calculate the area of both pans and divide. For example, converting from a 9-inch (23 cm) round (63.6 sq in / 410 sq cm) to a 9" × 13" (23 × 33 cm) rectangle (117 sq in / 755 sq cm): multiplier = 117 ÷ 63.6 = 1.84. Multiply all ingredients by 1.84, or roughly double the recipe and pour off 15% to avoid overfilling.

### What if I don't know my pan's volume?

Fill the pan with water from a measuring cup. Count the total cups — that's your pan's volume. This works for any pan shape, including irregular or vintage pans.

### Do I need to adjust baking time when I change pan size?

Yes. Larger pans spread the batter thinner, so they bake faster (reduce time by 5–10 minutes). Smaller pans have thicker batter and take longer (add 5–10 minutes). Deep pans like Bundts may need 10–15 extra minutes at a slightly lower temperature.

### Can I convert a Bundt recipe to round layer pans?

Yes. A standard 12-cup Bundt pan holds roughly the same volume as two 9-inch (23 cm) round layers. Split the Bundt recipe between two greased 9-inch (23 cm) rounds and reduce baking time by about 15 minutes.

### How do I handle eggs when the multiplier gives me a fraction?

Beat the egg and measure by volume: one large egg is about 3 tablespoons. So 0.5 of an egg = 1.5 tablespoons of beaten egg. For small fractions (under 0.5), you can usually just round to the nearest whole egg without problems.

### Why does my cake overflow when I use a different pan?

Your pan is too small for the amount of batter. Always fill cake pans no more than ⅔ to ¾ full to leave room for rising. If you have excess batter after filling to ¾, bake it separately as cupcakes.
