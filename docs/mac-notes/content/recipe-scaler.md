## How to Scale a Recipe: The Basic Math

At its core, scaling a recipe is multiplication. You calculate a **scaling factor** and multiply every ingredient by it.

### Finding Your Scaling Factor

```
Scaling Factor = Desired Servings ÷ Original Servings
```

**Examples:**
- Recipe makes 4 servings, you want 8: Scaling factor = 8 ÷ 4 = **2** (double everything)
- Recipe makes 6 servings, you want 3: Scaling factor = 3 ÷ 6 = **0.5** (halve everything)
- Recipe makes 4 servings, you want 6: Scaling factor = 6 ÷ 4 = **1.5**
- Recipe makes 12 servings, you want 8: Scaling factor = 8 ÷ 12 = **0.667** (two-thirds)

### Applying the Factor

Multiply every ingredient quantity by the scaling factor.

| Original (serves 4) | ×2 (serves 8) | ×0.5 (serves 2) | ×1.5 (serves 6) |
|---------------------|----------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| 2 cups flour | 4 cups | 1 cup | 3 cups |
| 1 cup sugar | 2 cups | ½ cup | 1½ cups |
| 3 eggs | 6 eggs | 1–2 eggs | 4–5 eggs |
| ½ tsp salt | 1 tsp | ¼ tsp | ¾ tsp |
| 1 tbsp vanilla | 2 tbsp | 1½ tsp | 1½ tbsp |
| 1 cup milk | 2 cups | ½ cup | 1½ cups |

Notice the eggs — you can't use 1.5 eggs. We'll solve that problem in detail below.

## The Egg Problem: What to Do with Fractional Eggs

This is the most common question people have when scaling recipes, and it's the reason we built the egg substitution chart into our calculator.

### The Quick Rule

- **Round down** for non-baking recipes (omelets, meatloaf, coating for fried chicken). One fewer egg rarely matters.
- **Round to the nearest whole egg** for baking, then fine-tune with the partial egg technique below.

### How to Use a Partial Egg

A large egg weighs approximately **50 grams** (without shell), with about 30g white and 20g yolk.

1. Crack the egg into a small bowl.
2. Beat it lightly with a fork until uniform.
3. Measure out the fraction you need by weight or volume.

| Fraction Needed | By Weight | By Volume (approximate) |
|----------------|-----------|------------------------|
| ¼ egg | 12–13g | ~1 tablespoon |
| ⅓ egg | 17g | ~1¼ tablespoons |
| ½ egg | 25g | ~1½ tablespoons |
| ⅔ egg | 33g | ~2 tablespoons |
| ¾ egg | 37–38g | ~2½ tablespoons |

### Complete Egg Scaling Quick-Reference Chart

This chart shows you exactly how many whole eggs to use and whether you need a partial egg.

| Original | ×0.5 | ×0.67 | ×1.5 | ×2 | ×3 | ×4 |
|----------|------|-------|------|-----|-----|-----|
| 1 egg | 1 egg* | 1 egg* | 1–2 eggs | 2 eggs | 3 eggs | 4 eggs |
| 2 eggs | 1 egg | 1–2 eggs | 3 eggs | 4 eggs | 6 eggs | 8 eggs |
| 3 eggs | 1–2 eggs | 2 eggs | 4–5 eggs | 6 eggs | 9 eggs | 12 eggs |
| 4 eggs | 2 eggs | 3 eggs | 6 eggs | 8 eggs | 12 eggs | 16 eggs |
| 5 eggs | 2–3 eggs | 3–4 eggs | 7–8 eggs | 10 eggs | 15 eggs | 20 eggs |

*When halving a recipe that calls for 1 egg, use the whole egg — using half an egg for most recipes introduces more hassle than benefit. The slight extra richness won't cause problems.*

### Egg Substitutions for Scaling

If you'd rather avoid dealing with partial eggs entirely, you can replace the egg with one of these per-egg substitutes:

| Substitute (per 1 egg) | Best For | Notes |
|------------------------|----------|-------|
| 1 tbsp ground flaxseed + 3 tbsp water (let sit 5 min) | Muffins, pancakes, cookies | Adds slight nuttiness. Vegan. |
| ¼ cup unsweetened applesauce | Quick breads, cakes | Adds moisture and mild sweetness. |
| ¼ cup mashed banana | Pancakes, muffins, brownies | Adds banana flavor — only use where that works. |
| 3 tbsp aquafaba (chickpea liquid) | Meringues, light cakes | Whips like egg whites. Vegan. |
| 1 tbsp cornstarch + 3 tbsp water | Binding in savory recipes | Good for meatloaf, veggie burgers. |
| 2 tbsp mayonnaise | Cakes, cupcakes | Adds richness and moisture. No one will taste it. |

## Scaling Baking Recipes: Why It's Different (and Harder)

Scaling savory cooking recipes — soups, stews, stir-fries, marinades — is almost always as simple as multiplying everything by the scaling factor. A little more or less garlic won't ruin your dinner.

Baking is different. Baking is chemistry, and the ratios between flour, leavening, fat, sugar, and liquid are precisely balanced. Here's what changes when you scale baking recipes beyond 2×.

### Leavening (Baking Powder & Baking Soda) — The Big One

**The rule for most scaling (up to 2×–3×):** Scale leavening agents proportionally — just multiply by the scaling factor like everything else.

**The rule for large-scale baking (4× and above):** Reduce leavening by about **20–25%** from the straight multiplication. Over-leavened large batches rise too fast, develop a coarse texture, and can collapse.

| Original | ×2 | ×3 | ×4 | ×5 |
|----------|-----|-----|-----|-----|
| 1 tsp baking powder | 2 tsp | 3 tsp | 3–3¼ tsp | 3¾–4 tsp |
| ½ tsp baking soda | 1 tsp | 1½ tsp | 1½ tsp | 1½–1¾ tsp |

**Why this happens:** In a larger batter volume, gas bubbles have farther to travel and more time to expand. The same proportional amount of leavening produces too much gas for the structure to support. Think of it like a balloon — twice the air in a much bigger balloon behaves differently than in the original small balloon.

### Salt and Spices

**Scale salt to about 75–80%** when doubling or more. A doubled recipe with doubled salt often tastes too salty because salt's perception scales non-linearly — our taste buds are more sensitive to salt in larger amounts. Start conservative and adjust.

**Spices and aromatics** — especially potent ones like cayenne, cinnamon, cloves, and nutmeg — should also be scaled conservatively. Start at 1.5× when doubling a recipe, taste, and add more.

| Ingredient Type | Scaling Approach |
|----------------|-----------------|
| Flour, sugar, butter, eggs | Scale directly (linear) |
| Liquid (water, milk, broth) | Scale directly, but check consistency |
| Baking powder / baking soda | Scale directly up to 3×; reduce 20–25% above 4× |
| Salt | Scale to 75–80% when doubling or more; taste and adjust |
| Strong spices (cayenne, cloves, cinnamon) | Start at 1.5× when doubling; add more to taste |
| Vanilla extract | Scale directly — it's forgiving |
| Yeast (bread recipes) | Scale directly up to 2×; at 3×+ reduce by 10–15% or extend rise time |

### Pan Size Matters

When you double a cake recipe, you can't just pour double the batter into the same pan. You need either a bigger pan or multiple pans of the same size.

**Quick pan volume guide:**
- 8" (20 cm) round pan holds about 4 cups of batter
- 9" (23 cm) round pan holds about 6 cups
- 9×13" (23×33 cm) rectangular holds about 14–15 cups
- For doubled cake recipes: use two pans of the original size, or one pan that holds double the volume

Check our **[Cake Pan Converter](/cake-pan-converter)** for exact pan-to-pan substitutions.

### Bake Time Adjustments

When you scale up a baking recipe but use a larger pan (or multiple pans), the bake time changes.

- **Same pan size, multiple pans:** Bake time stays roughly the same (you might need a few extra minutes if racks are close together).
- **Larger, deeper pan:** Increase bake time by 15–25%. A deeper layer of batter takes longer to cook through.
- **Scaling down to smaller pans:** Reduce bake time by 15–25%. Check early.

## Common Scaling Pitfalls (and How to Avoid Them)

### Pitfall 1: Scaling Cooking Liquids in Stovetop Recipes

When you double a soup or stew, you don't always need to double the liquid. The reason: evaporation doesn't double just because you doubled the volume. A pot of soup that simmers for an hour loses roughly the same amount of water to evaporation regardless of whether it started with 4 cups or 8 cups (assuming the same pot and heat level).

**Fix:** Scale liquid to about 1.5–1.75× when doubling a stovetop recipe. Add more as needed.

### Pitfall 2: Not Accounting for Cooking Vessel Size

Doubling a stir-fry doesn't work if your wok is already full. You'll end up steaming the food instead of searing it. Same for sheet pan dinners — overcrowded pans mean soggy vegetables.

**Fix:** Use a bigger vessel, or cook in batches.

### Pitfall 3: Ignoring Cooking Time Changes

A doubled stew in a bigger pot takes longer to come to a boil. A quadrupled casserole in a deep baking dish takes much longer to heat through in the center.

**Fix:** For scaled-up oven recipes, add 15–25% more time and use a thermometer or visual cues rather than relying on the original recipe's time.

### Pitfall 4: Scaling Gelatin, Pectin, and Thickeners Linearly

Gelatin strength doesn't scale linearly. If a recipe uses 1 packet of gelatin to set 2 cups of liquid, 2 packets won't set 4 cups with the same firmness — you might need 2.5 packets.

**Fix:** For gelatin, scale by about **1.25×** for every doubling. For cornstarch and flour-based thickeners, scale linearly.

### Pitfall 5: Converting Volume Measurements to Weight (Or Not)

When scaling, cups and tablespoons quickly become unwieldy. "7½ tablespoons of butter" is not a useful measurement. Converting to weight (grams or ounces) makes scaled recipes much more manageable. Our [Butter Converter](/butter-converter) can help with stick-to-gram conversions, and the [Cooking Measurement Converter](/cooking-measurement-converter) handles everything else.

**Fix:** Use a kitchen scale. Our calculator outputs both volume and weight where possible.

### Pitfall 6: Halving Recipes That Call for 1 Egg

A recipe that calls for 1 egg is already at its minimum — you can't use half an egg without effort. For most recipes, just use the whole egg when halving a 1-egg recipe. The extra richness is rarely a problem.

**Fix:** Use the whole egg, or substitute half the beaten egg as described in the egg section above.

## 6 Real-Life Scaling Examples

### Example 1: Doubling a Cookie Recipe

**Original (makes 24 cookies):** 2¼ cups flour, 1 cup butter, ¾ cup sugar, ¾ cup brown sugar, 2 eggs, 1 tsp vanilla, 1 tsp baking soda, 1 tsp salt, 2 cups chocolate chips.
**Doubled (makes 48 cookies):** 4½ cups flour, 2 cups butter, 1½ cups sugar, 1½ cups brown sugar, 4 eggs, 2 tsp vanilla, 2 tsp baking soda, 2 tsp salt, 4 cups chocolate chips.
**Notes:** This one's straightforward — cookies scale linearly in most cases. Same bake temp, same time, just bake more batches.

### Example 2: Halving a Chili Recipe (Odd Eggs)

**Original (serves 8):** 2 lbs ground beef, 1 can (28 oz) crushed tomatoes, 2 cans kidney beans, 1 large onion, 3 cloves garlic, 2 tbsp chili powder, 1 tsp cumin, 1 egg, salt to taste.
**Halved (serves 4):** 1 lb ground beef, 1 can (14 oz) crushed tomatoes, 1 can kidney beans, ½ large onion, 2 cloves garlic (round up), 1 tbsp chili powder, ½ tsp cumin, 1 egg (use the whole egg), salt to taste.
**Notes:** Use the whole egg — half an egg in chili isn't worth the effort. Round garlic up because half a clove is hard to mince.

### Example 3: Scaling Banana Bread from 1 Loaf to 3 Loaves

**Original (1 loaf):** 3 bananas, ⅓ cup melted butter, ¾ cup sugar, 1 egg, 1 tsp vanilla, 1 tsp baking soda, pinch salt, 1½ cups flour.
**Tripled (3 loaves):** 9 bananas, 1 cup melted butter, 2¼ cups sugar, 3 eggs, 1 tbsp vanilla, 2½ tsp baking soda (reduced from 3 tsp — see leavening section), ¼ tsp salt, 4½ cups flour.
**Notes:** We reduced the baking soda slightly from the straight 3× (3 tsp) to 2½ tsp. This prevents over-rising. Bake each loaf in its own pan at the same temperature, and add 5–10 minutes to bake time if pans are close together on oven racks.

### Example 4: Scaling a Pasta Dish from 6 Servings to 2

**Original (serves 6):** 1 lb pasta, 3 cups marinara sauce, ½ cup Parmesan, 2 cloves garlic, 2 tbsp olive oil, salt and pepper.
**Scaled to 2 servings (factor 0.33):** ⅓ lb pasta (about 5 oz), 1 cup marinara sauce, 2–3 tbsp Parmesan, 1 clove garlic, 2 tsp olive oil, salt and pepper to taste.
**Notes:** This is straightforward cooking math — no baking chemistry to worry about.

### Example 5: Quadrupling a Cake Recipe for a Party

**Original (one 9" layer):** 1½ cups flour, 1 cup sugar, ½ cup butter, 2 eggs, ¾ cup milk, 2 tsp baking powder, ½ tsp salt, 1 tsp vanilla.
**Quadrupled (four 9" layers):** 6 cups flour, 4 cups sugar, 2 cups butter, 8 eggs, 3 cups milk, 2 tbsp + 1 tsp baking powder (reduced from 8 tsp), 1½ tsp salt (reduced from 2 tsp), 4 tsp vanilla.
**Notes:** Baking powder reduced by ~20% from the straight 4× amount. Salt reduced to ~75% of the 4× amount. Mix the batter in two batches if your mixer can't handle the full volume. Bake two pans at a time.

### Example 6: Converting a Recipe from 4 Servings to 7 (Awkward Scaling)

**Original (serves 4):** 1 cup rice, 2 cups chicken broth, 1 lb chicken thighs, 1 tsp garlic powder, ½ tsp pepper, 1 tbsp soy sauce.
**Scaled to 7 servings (factor 1.75):** 1¾ cups rice, 3½ cups broth, 1¾ lb chicken thighs, 1¾ tsp garlic powder, ¾ tsp pepper (round from 0.875), 1¾ tbsp soy sauce.
**Notes:** The awkward fractions are why a calculator is helpful. In practice, you'd round the garlic powder to 2 tsp, the pepper to 1 tsp, and call it close enough.

## Pro Tips for Scaling Recipes Successfully

**Tip 1: Use weight, not volume.** "3.5 cups of flour" is imprecise because different people scoop differently. "490 grams of flour" is exact. If you're scaling recipes regularly, invest in a kitchen scale — it's the single best tool for accurate scaling.

**Tip 2: Don't scale cooking time linearly.** A doubled casserole doesn't take twice as long — it takes maybe 25% longer if you're using a deeper dish, or the same time if you're using two pans side by side.

**Tip 3: Taste as you go for seasonings.** Salt, spices, acid (lemon juice, vinegar), and sugar are all perception-dependent. Scale them conservatively (75% of the calculated amount) and adjust to taste.

**Tip 4: When in doubt, make two batches.** If you're quadrupling a cake recipe for the first time, it's often safer to make the batter twice in two separate bowls rather than trying to mix 4× in one giant batch. The mixing dynamics change when you scale up, and under-mixed or over-mixed spots are more likely in a huge batch.

**Tip 5: Write it down.** Before you start cooking, write out the full scaled recipe. It's surprisingly easy to forget whether you already added the doubled amount or the original amount when you're mid-recipe and your hands are covered in flour.

**Tip 6: Scale in metric for baking.** If you have a recipe in cups and you're scaling by an awkward factor like 1.5× or 0.67×, convert to grams first. "225g flour" is easier to work with than "1½ cups minus 1 tablespoon."

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I just double everything in a recipe?
For most cooking recipes (soups, stews, sautés, salads), yes — doubling works perfectly. For baking recipes, you can double most ingredients directly, but you may want to slightly reduce leavening agents (baking powder and baking soda) and salt. See the scaling adjustments section above for details.

### What do I do when a halved recipe calls for half an egg?
You have three options: (1) Use the whole egg — in most recipes, one extra half-egg won't cause problems. (2) Beat the egg in a bowl and measure out half by volume (~1½ tablespoons). (3) Use a substitute like 2 tablespoons of mayonnaise, ⅛ cup of applesauce, or half a flax egg.

### Why does my doubled cake recipe taste different?
The most common reasons are over-leavened batter (too much baking powder causes coarse texture or collapse), over-salted batter, or uneven mixing in a large batch. When scaling baking recipes above 2×, reduce leavening by 20–25% and salt by 15–20%. Mix in smaller batches when possible.

### How do I scale a recipe by thirds?
Multiply every ingredient by 0.333 (one-third) or 0.667 (two-thirds). For example, to make one-third of a recipe that calls for 1½ cups flour: 1.5 × 0.333 = 0.5 cups. Our calculator handles these fractions and converts them to practical kitchen measurements.

### Can I scale bread and yeast recipes?
Yes, but yeast needs special attention at large scales. Up to 3×, scale yeast linearly. Above 3×, reduce yeast by 10–15% and extend the rise time slightly. The reason: more dough retains heat better during fermentation, which makes yeast more active. Too much yeast in a large batch leads to over-proofing.

### Do I need to adjust cooking temperature when scaling?
Generally no — the oven temperature stays the same regardless of recipe scale. What changes is the cooking time: larger volumes in deeper pans need more time; same-sized portions on multiple sheet pans need the same time. Always use a thermometer or visual cues instead of relying solely on a timer.

### What kitchen tools help with scaling?
A digital kitchen scale (grams and ounces) is the most important. Beyond that: a set of measuring cups and spoons, a calculator or scaling app, and a notepad to write out the scaled recipe before you start. For baking, a bench scraper helps divide dough into equal portions.

### Is there a limit to how much I can scale a recipe?
In practical terms, yes. Most recipes work well scaled from 0.5× to 4×. Beyond that, you may run into issues with mixing capacity, bake time adjustments, leavening ratios, and cooking vessel sizes. If you need to scale 10× or more (e.g., for a catering event), it's better to make multiple standard batches than one giant batch.
