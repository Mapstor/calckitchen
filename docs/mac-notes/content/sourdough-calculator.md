# Sourdough Calculator — Starter Feeding & Bread Recipe Calculator

Sourdough baking is all about ratios. Whether you're feeding your starter, building a levain, or calculating a bread recipe, the math determines everything — how fast your starter peaks, what your crumb looks like, and whether your loaf has the perfect balance of tangy flavor and airy structure. Our **Sourdough Calculator** handles both sides: a **Starter Feeding Calculator** for maintaining your culture, and a **Bread Recipe Calculator** for dialing in your loaves.

If you're new to sourdough, don't let the percentages intimidate you. This guide breaks down the math in plain language and gives you the tools to bake with confidence.

---

## Mode 1: Starter Feeding Calculator

Your sourdough starter is a living culture of wild yeast and bacteria. To keep it alive and active, you feed it regularly with flour and water in specific ratios. The ratio you choose determines how quickly the starter peaks (reaches maximum rise) and how much you'll have on hand for baking.

### Understanding Feeding Ratios

Feeding ratios are expressed as **starter : flour : water** by weight. Here's how the common ratios work:

| Ratio | Example (20g starter) | Peak Time (75°F / 24°C) | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1:1:1** | 20g starter + 20g flour + 20g water | 4–6 hours | Quick builds, daily maintenance, warm environments |
| **1:2:2** | 20g starter + 40g flour + 40g water | 8–10 hours | Standard maintenance, overnight feeds |
| **1:3:3** | 20g starter + 60g flour + 60g water | 10–14 hours | Slow, controlled rise; before long absences |
| **1:5:5** | 20g starter + 100g flour + 100g water | 14–18 hours | Pre-vacation feed; very slow build |
| **1:10:10** | 20g starter + 200g flour + 200g water | 18–24+ hours | Reviving neglected starters |

The first number is the amount of existing starter you keep. The second and third numbers are the flour and water you add. A **1:1:1** ratio means equal parts of each — the yeast-heavy starter overwhelms the fresh flour quickly, so it peaks fast. A **1:5:5** ratio means the small amount of starter has much more food to consume, so it takes much longer to peak.

### Which Ratio Should You Use?

**For daily room-temperature maintenance:** Use **1:1:1** or **1:2:2**. Feed once every 12 hours (1:1:1) or once every 24 hours (1:2:2). Your starter should roughly double in volume between feedings.

**For fridge storage:** Feed at **1:2:2** or **1:3:3**, then immediately refrigerate. The cold slows fermentation dramatically. Feed once every 5–7 days. Pull from the fridge, let it sit at room temperature for 2 hours, discard all but a tablespoon, feed, and return to the fridge.

**For baking day — building a levain:** Most bakers build a separate levain (a portion of starter grown specifically for a recipe) the night before baking. Use **1:2:2** or **1:3:3** for an overnight levain that peaks in 8–12 hours.

### Starter Feeding Calculator Formula

This is straightforward multiplication:

**Flour = Starter amount × flour ratio number**
**Water = Starter amount × water ratio number**

For 1:3:3 with 25g starter: Flour = 25 × 3 = 75g. Water = 25 × 3 = 75g. Total = 175g.

### Flour Types for Starter Feeding

| Flour Type | Effect on Starter |
|---|---|
| All-purpose white | Standard choice. Predictable, moderate activity. |
| Bread flour | Slightly more food for microbes. Marginally more active. |
| Whole wheat | More active — the bran contains extra nutrients and microbes. Starter peaks faster. |
| Rye | Most active of all. Excellent for reviving sluggish starters. |
| 50/50 white + whole wheat | Popular compromise — more activity than white, less sour than 100% whole grain. |

Many experienced bakers maintain their starter on all-purpose flour for consistency and predictability, then build their levain with a flour blend that matches the bread recipe.

## Mode 2: Bread Recipe Calculator

The bread recipe calculator takes your desired flour weight, hydration percentage, starter percentage, and salt percentage, and calculates the exact weight of every ingredient.

### Default Sourdough Bread Recipe (1 loaf)

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Flour | 100% | 500g |
| Water | 70% | 350g |
| Starter (100% hydration) | 20% | 100g |
| Salt | 2% | 10g |
| **Total dough weight** | — | **960g** |

This produces a standard 2-pound sourdough boule or batard with a moderately open crumb, mild tang, and good oven spring.

### The Formula

Starting from the total flour weight:

- **Water** = Flour × (hydration% ÷ 100)
- **Starter** = Flour × (starter% ÷ 100)
- **Salt** = Flour × (salt% ÷ 100)
- **Total dough** = Flour + Water + Starter + Salt

### Adjustable Parameters

**Flour weight (g):** This is your base number. For a single standard loaf, 450–500g flour is typical. For two loaves, use 900–1000g.

| Flour Weight | Typical Loaf Size |
|---|---|
| 300g | Small boule (1.2 lb dough) |
| 450g | Medium loaf (1.8 lb dough) |
| 500g | Standard loaf (2 lb dough) |
| 750g | Large loaf or 2 small loaves |
| 1000g | 2 standard loaves |

**Hydration (%):** The percentage of water relative to flour. This is the most impactful variable you'll adjust. See the hydration section below for detailed guidance.

**Starter/inoculation (%):** How much starter you add relative to the flour weight. This controls fermentation speed.

| Starter % | Fermentation Speed | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| 10% | Very slow (8–10 hours bulk) | Warm environments (80°F+ / 27°C+), complex flavor development |
| 15% | Slow-medium (6–8 hours bulk) | Warm kitchens, longer schedules |
| 20% | Standard (4–6 hours bulk at 75°F / 24°C) | Default for most recipes and environments |
| 25% | Medium-fast (3–5 hours bulk) | Cooler kitchens (68–72°F / 20–22°C) |
| 30%+ | Fast (2–4 hours bulk) | Cold environments, quick bakes, or when starter is sluggish |

**Salt (%):** Almost always 2% of flour weight. Salt controls fermentation rate, strengthens gluten, and adds flavor. Going below 1.8% makes bread taste flat; above 2.5% can inhibit yeast activity.

## Understanding Hydration: A Beginner's Guide

Hydration is the single most discussed — and most confusing — variable in sourdough baking. It simply means: **how much water is in the dough, expressed as a percentage of the flour weight.**

If you use 500g flour and 350g water, your hydration is 350 ÷ 500 = **70%**.

### What Hydration Does to Your Bread

| Hydration | Dough Feel | Crumb Structure | Crust | Difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 60–65% | Firm, easy to shape | Tight, even crumb (sandwich bread) | Thick | Beginner-friendly |
| 65–70% | Slightly tacky, manageable | Moderate openness | Medium | Intermediate |
| 70–75% | Sticky, requires technique | Open, irregular holes | Thin, crackly | Intermediate-Advanced |
| 75–80% | Very wet, slack | Very open, large holes | Thin, blistered | Advanced |
| 80%+ | Batter-like | Extremely open (ciabatta-style) | Very thin | Expert |

**For beginners:** Start at **65–68% hydration**. This gives you a dough that's firm enough to shape without frustration while still producing good oven spring and a pleasant crumb. You can increase by 2–3% each bake as you get comfortable with the dough's behavior.

**Why higher hydration is harder:** Wet doughs are slack and sticky. They spread instead of holding shape. They stick to your hands, your bench, your banneton. Shaping them requires confidence and speed — the more you handle them, the stickier they get. But higher-hydration doughs produce more steam inside the loaf, creating larger air pockets and a more dramatic, open crumb.

### The Hydration Confusion: Starter Contributes Water and Flour

Here's something that trips up many bakers: **your starter is itself a mixture of flour and water.** A 100% hydration starter (the most common kind, fed with equal weights flour and water) is 50% flour and 50% water by weight.

This means 100g of starter adds 50g of flour and 50g of water to your dough. The **true hydration** of your dough is:

**True hydration = (Water + starter water) ÷ (Flour + starter flour) × 100**

For the default recipe (500g flour, 350g water, 100g starter at 100% hydration):

True hydration = (350 + 50) ÷ (500 + 50) × 100 = 400 ÷ 550 × 100 = **72.7%**

The recipe says 70% hydration, but the actual dough hydration is 72.7% once you account for the water in the starter. This is why a sourdough recipe at "70% hydration" feels wetter than a commercial yeast recipe at 70% — the starter adds extra water.

**Most sourdough recipes quote hydration exclusive of the starter contribution**, which is simpler and is what our calculator uses by default. But if you want to know your true dough hydration, the calculator shows this as well.

## Building a Levain

A levain (sometimes spelled "leaven") is a portion of starter that you grow specifically for a bake. Instead of taking starter directly from your maintenance jar, you build a levain with a controlled ratio to ensure it peaks at exactly the right time.

### Standard Levain Build

The night before baking (typically 8–12 hours ahead):

| Component | Amount | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Mature starter | 20g | From your maintenance jar |
| Flour | 50g | Same type you'll use in bread, or a blend |
| Water | 50g | Room temperature |
| **Total levain** | **120g** | Will be ready in 8–12 hours at 72–76°F (22–24°C) |

This produces 120g of levain at 100% hydration. If your recipe calls for 100g of starter, build 120g to account for what sticks to the container.

### How to Tell When Your Levain Is Ready

Your levain is ready when it has roughly **doubled in volume** and passes the **float test**: drop a small spoonful into a glass of water. If it floats, the levain has trapped enough gas to be active and ready. If it sinks, give it more time.

Other signs: the surface should be domed (not flat or sunken), and you should see bubbles throughout. A levain that has collapsed or looks concave has passed its peak — it's over-fermented. You can still use it, but the bread may be more sour and have less oven spring. If possible, build a new one.

## Sourdough Baking Timeline

Here's a typical schedule for a standard sourdough loaf using the default recipe (500g flour, 70% hydration, 20% starter):

| Time | Step | Details |
|---|---|---|
| **9:00 PM (night before)** | Build levain | 20g starter + 50g flour + 50g water. Cover, leave at room temp. |
| **8:00 AM** | Autolyse | Mix 500g flour + 325g water (hold back 25g). Rest 30–60 min. |
| **9:00 AM** | Mix final dough | Add 100g levain + 10g salt + reserved 25g water. Mix until incorporated. |
| **9:30 AM – 1:30 PM** | Bulk fermentation | 4 hours at 75°F. Perform 3–4 sets of stretch and folds every 30 min for the first 2 hours, then leave undisturbed. |
| **1:30 PM** | Pre-shape | Gently turn dough onto bench, shape into loose round. Rest 20 min. |
| **2:00 PM** | Final shape | Shape into boule or batard. Place seam-down in floured banneton. |
| **2:00 PM** | Cold retard | Cover banneton, refrigerate 12–16 hours (overnight). |
| **Next morning** | Bake | Preheat Dutch oven to 500°F (260°C). Score cold dough, bake 20 min covered + 20–25 min uncovered at 450°F (232°C). |

This "same-day mix, overnight retard" schedule is the most popular because it fits around a normal life. You do the active work during one day, then the fridge does the slow fermentation work overnight while you sleep.

## 6 Real-Life Examples

### Example 1: First Sourdough Loaf (Beginner)

You just got your starter active and want to bake your very first loaf. Keep it simple.

**Settings:** 450g flour, 65% hydration, 20% starter, 2% salt

| Ingredient | Weight |
|---|---|
| Bread flour | 450g |
| Water | 293g |
| Starter (100% hydration) | 90g |
| Salt | 9g |
| **Total dough** | **842g** |

Low hydration makes this dough easy to handle. Shape it into a boule, proof in a floured bowl (you don't need a banneton for your first bake), and bake in a Dutch oven. Focus on learning the process before chasing open crumb.

### Example 2: Standard Weekday Loaf

Your go-to recipe that fits around a work schedule. Mix in the evening, bake the next morning.

**Settings:** 500g flour, 72% hydration, 20% starter, 2% salt

| Ingredient | Weight |
|---|---|
| Bread flour (400g) + whole wheat (100g) | 500g total |
| Water | 360g |
| Starter | 100g |
| Salt | 10g |
| **Total dough** | **970g** |

The 20% whole wheat adds flavor complexity and nutrition. Mix at 6 PM, bulk ferment until 10 PM (stretch and folds during the first 2 hours), shape, into banneton, fridge overnight. Bake at 6 AM before work.

### Example 3: High-Hydration Open Crumb (Advanced)

You want those Instagram-worthy big holes and a thin, crackly crust.

**Settings:** 500g flour, 80% hydration, 18% starter, 2% salt

| Ingredient | Weight |
|---|---|
| Bread flour | 500g |
| Water | 400g |
| Starter | 90g |
| Salt | 10g |
| **Total dough** | **1000g** |

This dough will be extremely wet and slack. Use coil folds instead of stretch and folds. Handle minimally. Shape quickly with a wet bench knife and lightly floured hands. This dough benefits from a longer bulk ferment (5–6 hours) and an overnight cold retard for better structure.

### Example 4: Two Loaves for the Weekend

You're baking for the family and want two standard loaves.

**Settings:** 1000g flour, 70% hydration, 20% starter, 2% salt

| Ingredient | Weight |
|---|---|
| Bread flour | 1000g |
| Water | 700g |
| Starter | 200g |
| Salt | 20g |
| **Total dough** | **1920g** (~960g per loaf) |

Build a levain of 240g (to account for container residue): 30g starter + 105g flour + 105g water the night before. Divide the dough after bulk fermentation, shape into two boules, and cold retard both. Bake one in the morning and one in the afternoon (or freeze one after the cold retard for up to 2 weeks — bake directly from frozen, adding 5 minutes to the covered bake time).

### Example 5: Feeding Starter After Vacation (Revival)

Your starter has been in the fridge for 3 weeks and looks gray and liquid-y on top (that's hooch — alcohol produced by hungry microbes). It's still alive.

**Feeding plan:** Pour off the hooch. Take 10g of starter and discard the rest. Feed at **1:10:10**: 10g starter + 100g flour + 100g water. Let it sit at room temperature. It may take 18–24 hours for the first sign of life. Feed again at **1:5:5** once it shows bubbles. After 2–3 feedings at 1:5:5 or 1:3:3, your starter should be doubling reliably and passing the float test. Then you're ready to bake.

### Example 6: Starter Maintenance — Minimizing Waste

You hate discarding starter. Here's a minimal-waste maintenance routine.

**Keep only 10g of starter at each feeding.** Feed at 1:2:2 for a total of 50g after feeding. This tiny amount is enough to maintain your culture and build a levain when you need it. You discard just 40g at each feeding (or use it in discard recipes like pancakes, crackers, or waffles). When you want to bake, build a levain from your maintenance jar 12 hours ahead.

## Pro Tips for Sourdough Success

**Temperature is your fermentation remote control.** A dough at 80°F (27°C) ferments roughly twice as fast as one at 70°F (21°C). If your dough is fermenting too slowly, find a warmer spot (inside the oven with just the light on works great — it's typically 78–82°F / 26–28°C). If it's going too fast, use cooler water and find a cool spot.

**Don't rely on time — rely on observation.** "Bulk ferment for 4 hours" is a rough guideline, not a rule. Your dough is ready for shaping when it has increased in volume by 50–75%, feels airy and jiggly, and has visible bubbles throughout. In a warm kitchen this could be 3 hours; in a cool kitchen, 6 hours.

**Use the "aliquot jar" method.** Take a small piece of dough during mixing and place it in a small, straight-sided jar with a rubber band marking the starting level. This makes it easy to see exactly how much the dough has risen — much easier than eyeballing a mass of dough in a bowl.

**Master shaping before increasing hydration.** Good shaping technique (building surface tension without degassing) is what gives your loaf height and ear. Practice at 65–70% until you can confidently shape a tight boule, then start pushing hydration upward.

**Score with confidence.** A timid, shallow score doesn't open properly. Use a sharp razor blade (lame) held at a 30–45° angle and make one swift, decisive cut about ¼ inch deep. The angle creates the "ear" — that dramatic flap of crust that sourdough bakers chase.

**Steam is everything for crust.** The first 15–20 minutes of baking need steam to keep the crust flexible while the loaf expands. A Dutch oven traps steam naturally. If baking on a stone, use a steam pan with boiling water, ice cubes, or lava rocks.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What hydration is my starter?

If you feed your starter equal weights of flour and water (e.g., 50g flour + 50g water), it's a **100% hydration starter** — the most common type. If you feed 50g flour + 40g water, it's an 80% hydration starter (stiffer). Most recipes assume 100% hydration.

### How much starter should I use in sourdough bread?

The standard is **20% of flour weight** (also called 20% inoculation). For 500g of flour, that's 100g of starter. Use less (10–15%) for slower fermentation and more complex flavor, or more (25–30%) for faster fermentation in cold environments.

### Can I use starter straight from the fridge?

Technically yes, but it won't be at peak activity. For best results, pull the starter from the fridge, feed it once (1:2:2), wait until it doubles (6–10 hours), then use it or build a levain from it. Some experienced bakers use unfed fridge starter for more sour flavor.

### What's the difference between starter and levain?

A starter is your ongoing culture that you maintain indefinitely. A levain is a portion of starter that you build up specifically for a single bake, typically the night before. You take a small amount of starter, feed it, let it peak, and add it to your bread dough. The remaining starter in your jar continues its normal maintenance schedule.

### Why is my sourdough too sour (or not sour enough)?

**More sour:** Use a higher percentage of whole grain flour, ferment longer, use a smaller inoculation (10–15%), ferment at cooler temperatures, and include a long cold retard. Acetic acid (vinegar-like sourness) develops more at cooler temperatures.

**Less sour:** Use mostly white flour, use more starter (25–30%) for a faster ferment, ferment at warm temperatures (78–82°F / 26–28°C), and skip the cold retard. Lactic acid (mild, yogurt-like tang) dominates at warmer temperatures.

### What's the float test and is it reliable?

The float test involves dropping a spoonful of starter (or levain) into water. If it floats, it's ready. The test works because active starter is full of gas bubbles, making it less dense than water. It's reasonably reliable for 100% hydration starters, but stiffer starters (less than 80% hydration) may not float even when fully active because they're denser. In that case, rely on volume (has it doubled?) and visual cues (bubbly, domed surface).

### How long does sourdough starter last in the fridge?

A healthy starter can survive 1–2 weeks in the fridge without feeding. After 2–3 weeks, it will be sluggish but recoverable with 2–3 feedings over a few days. After a month or more, it may need 4–5 feedings to fully revive. Starters are remarkably resilient — even very neglected ones can usually be brought back to life.
