# Baker's Percentage Calculator — Understand and Use Baker's Math

If you've ever read a bread recipe and seen something like "65% hydration" or "2% salt," you've encountered **baker's percentages** — the universal language of professional baking. Our **Baker's Percentage Calculator** works in both directions: enter baker's percentages to get exact ingredient weights, or enter an existing recipe's weights to discover its percentages. It's both a practical tool and an educational resource for anyone who wants to truly understand how bread formulas work.

Once you understand baker's percentages, you'll never look at a bread recipe the same way. You'll be able to compare any two recipes at a glance, scale with confidence, and adjust hydration, salt, or enrichments knowing exactly what effect they'll have.

---

## What Are Baker's Percentages?

Baker's percentages (also called baker's math or formula percentages) are a system where **every ingredient is expressed as a percentage of the total flour weight.** Flour is always 100%. Everything else is relative to flour.

This is different from true percentages, where all ingredients would add up to 100%. In baker's math, the percentages typically add up to 160–200% or more for enriched doughs.

### Basic Example: Simple Bread

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Weight (for 500g flour) |
|---|---|---|
| Flour | 100% | 500g |
| Water | 65% | 325g |
| Salt | 2% | 10g |
| Yeast | 1% | 5g |
| **Total** | **168%** | **840g** |

Reading this: the water is 65% of the flour weight, the salt is 2% of the flour weight, and so on. Total dough weight is 168% of the flour weight, or 840g.

### Why Not Just Use Weights?

Baker's percentages solve three problems that raw weights don't:

**Instant recipe comparison.** If someone says their bread is "72% hydration with 2% salt and 20% starter," any baker immediately understands the character of that dough — it's a moderately wet sourdough. If they just said "360g water, 10g salt, 100g starter," you'd need to know the flour weight and do mental math to understand the recipe.

**Effortless scaling.** Want to make 3 loaves instead of 1? Just multiply the flour weight by 3, and every other ingredient scales proportionally. The percentages never change — they're the recipe's DNA.

**Universal communication.** A recipe in baker's percentages works regardless of units. Whether you measure in grams, ounces, or pounds, the ratios stay the same. A 65% hydration bread is a 65% hydration bread in any unit system.

## The Two Directions of Baker's Math

### Direction 1: Percentages → Weights (Forward Calculation)

You know the baker's percentages and the amount of flour you want to use. Calculate the weight of every ingredient.

**Formula:**

**Ingredient weight = Flour weight × (Baker's percentage ÷ 100)**

**Example:** You want to make bread with 750g flour at 68% hydration, 2% salt, and 0.8% instant yeast.

- Water = 750 × (68 ÷ 100) = **510g**
- Salt = 750 × (2 ÷ 100) = **15g**
- Yeast = 750 × (0.8 ÷ 100) = **6g**
- Total dough = 750 + 510 + 15 + 6 = **1,281g**

### Direction 2: Weights → Percentages (Reverse Calculation)

You have a recipe in weights and want to know its baker's percentages. This lets you understand any recipe's character and compare it to others.

**Formula:**

**Baker's percentage = (Ingredient weight ÷ Flour weight) × 100**

**Example:** You found a recipe that calls for 400g flour, 280g water, 8g salt, and 4g yeast. What are the baker's percentages?

- Water: (280 ÷ 400) × 100 = **70%**
- Salt: (8 ÷ 400) × 100 = **2%**
- Yeast: (4 ÷ 400) × 100 = **1%**

Now you know this is a 70% hydration dough — a moderately wet bread dough.

### Bonus Direction: Target Dough Weight → All Ingredients

Sometimes you know how much total dough you need (for example, you need 1,000g of dough for two 500g loaves). From the baker's percentages, you can calculate the flour weight first, then everything else.

**Formula:**

**Flour weight = Target total dough weight ÷ (Sum of all percentages ÷ 100)**

**Example:** You need 1,000g of dough using a formula of 100% flour, 65% water, 2% salt, 1% yeast (total = 168%).

- Flour = 1,000 ÷ (168 ÷ 100) = 1,000 ÷ 1.68 = **595g**
- Water = 595 × 0.65 = **387g**
- Salt = 595 × 0.02 = **12g**
- Yeast = 595 × 0.01 = **6g**
- Total: 595 + 387 + 12 + 6 = **1,000g** ✓

## Working with Multiple Flours

When a recipe uses more than one type of flour (common in sourdough and artisan breads), all flours together equal 100%. Each flour's baker's percentage represents its share of the total flour.

### Example: Country Loaf with 3 Flours

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Weight (for 1000g total flour) |
|---|---|---|
| Bread flour | 70% | 700g |
| Whole wheat flour | 20% | 200g |
| Rye flour | 10% | 100g |
| **Total flour** | **100%** | **1,000g** |
| Water | 72% | 720g |
| Salt | 2% | 20g |
| Starter | 20% | 200g |
| **Total dough** | **194%** | **1,940g** |

The flour percentages (70 + 20 + 10) must add up to 100%. Every other ingredient is calculated against the total flour weight (1,000g), not against any individual flour.

### Flour Contributions from Pre-Ferments and Starters

This is where baker's math gets slightly more complex. A sourdough starter at 100% hydration contains equal parts flour and water. A poolish also contains equal parts flour and water. This flour and water are part of the total formula.

**Comprehensive formula approach:**

| Component | Flour | Water | Starter | Salt |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Levain build | 50g | 50g | (from seed) | — |
| Final dough mix | 450g | 310g | — | 10g |
| **Total formula** | **500g** | **360g** | — | **10g** |
| **Baker's %** | **100%** | **72%** | **20% (as added)** | **2%** |

The 100g of levain contributes 50g flour and 50g water to the total. So the actual flour in the dough is 500g (450 + 50), and the actual water is 360g (310 + 50), giving a true hydration of 72%.

## 6 Real-Life Examples

### Example 1: Convert a Cup-Based Recipe to Baker's Percentages

You found a bread recipe online that uses cups: 3 cups flour, 1¼ cups water, 1 tsp salt, ½ tsp yeast. Converting to weights first (1 cup flour ≈ 130g):

- Flour: 3 cups × 130g = 390g
- Water: 1.25 cups × 237ml = 296g
- Salt: 1 tsp ≈ 6g
- Yeast: 0.5 tsp ≈ 1.6g

**Baker's percentages:**
- Hydration: 296 ÷ 390 × 100 = **75.9%**
- Salt: 6 ÷ 390 × 100 = **1.5%**
- Yeast: 1.6 ÷ 390 × 100 = **0.4%**

Now you can see this is a high-hydration dough (76%) with low salt (1.5% — most bakers prefer 2%). You might increase salt to 8g (2%) for better flavor and gluten strength.

### Example 2: Scale a Recipe from 2 Loaves to 5 Loaves

Your recipe: 100% flour (500g), 68% water, 2% salt, 0.8% yeast. Makes 2 loaves.

For 5 loaves, multiply flour by 2.5 (from 2 loaves to 5 loaves): 500g × 2.5 = 1,250g flour.

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Flour | 100% | 1,250g |
| Water | 68% | 850g |
| Salt | 2% | 25g |
| Yeast | 0.8% | 10g |
| **Total** | **170.8%** | **2,135g** |

The percentages never change. You just adjust the flour weight and recalculate.

### Example 3: Compare Two Sourdough Recipes

Recipe A calls for 500g flour, 375g water, 100g starter, 10g salt. Recipe B calls for 800g flour, 520g water, 120g starter, 16g salt. Which is wetter? Which uses more starter?

**Recipe A percentages:** Water 75%, Starter 20%, Salt 2%.
**Recipe B percentages:** Water 65%, Starter 15%, Salt 2%.

Recipe A is significantly wetter (75% vs. 65%) and uses more starter (20% vs. 15%), meaning it will ferment faster and have a more open crumb. The salt is identical in both. Without baker's percentages, comparing these recipes would require mental gymnastics.

### Example 4: Design a Pizza Dough from Scratch

You want a NY-style pizza dough: moderate hydration (63%), some olive oil (3%), a touch of sugar (1.5%), standard salt (2.5%), and instant yeast (0.7%). Making 4 pizzas at 300g each.

**Step 1:** Total dough needed = 4 × 300 = 1,200g
**Step 2:** Sum of percentages = 100 + 63 + 2.5 + 0.7 + 3 + 1.5 = 170.7%
**Step 3:** Flour = 1,200 ÷ 1.707 = 703g

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Bread flour | 100% | 703g |
| Water | 63% | 443g |
| Salt | 2.5% | 18g |
| Yeast (instant) | 0.7% | 5g |
| Olive oil | 3% | 21g |
| Sugar | 1.5% | 11g |
| **Total** | **170.7%** | **~1,200g** |

### Example 5: Adjust Hydration on an Existing Recipe

Your current bread recipe (500g flour, 65% hydration) makes a nice but slightly dense loaf. You want to increase hydration to 72% for a more open crumb.

Current water: 500 × 0.65 = 325g.
New water: 500 × 0.72 = 360g.

Just add 35g more water. Everything else stays the same. Baker's percentages make this kind of adjustment trivial — change one number, recalculate one ingredient.

### Example 6: Reverse-Engineer a Bakery's Formula

You bought an amazing loaf from a local bakery and they told you it's "78% hydration, 18% levain, 12% whole wheat, 2% salt." From just these numbers, you can recreate the recipe.

For a 500g flour base:
- Bread flour: 88% (440g)
- Whole wheat: 12% (60g)
- Water: 78% (390g)
- Levain: 18% (90g)
- Salt: 2% (10g)
- Total: ~990g

You now have a complete, scalable recipe from just four percentages.

## Common Mistakes with Baker's Percentages

**Mistake 1: Confusing baker's % with true %.** Baker's percentages don't add up to 100%. A recipe with 100% flour + 65% water + 2% salt = 167%. This is correct. In true percentages, those same ingredients would be approximately 60% flour, 39% water, 1.2% salt. Baker's math always uses flour as the baseline.

**Mistake 2: Forgetting pre-ferment contributions.** If your recipe calls for 20% poolish and 65% water, the true hydration is higher than 65% because the poolish itself contains water. Always account for the flour and water inside pre-ferments when calculating true hydration.

**Mistake 3: Not counting all flours.** If your recipe uses 400g bread flour and 100g whole wheat, the total flour is 500g (100%). The whole wheat is 20% of the total flour, not a separate ingredient. Water at 65% means 65% of 500g = 325g, not 65% of 400g.

**Mistake 4: Treating salt as trivial.** The difference between 1.8% and 2.5% salt seems small, but it's nearly 40% more salt by weight. In a 500g flour recipe, that's the difference between 9g and 12.5g — very noticeable in flavor and fermentation speed.

## Pro Tips

**Always weigh, never measure by volume.** Baker's percentages only work when you use weight measurements. A "cup" of flour can vary by 30% depending on how it's scooped. A gram is a gram.

**Keep a baking journal in percentages.** Record every bake as baker's percentages plus notes on the result. Over time, you'll develop intuition: "72% hydration with my flour gives me the crumb I like" or "0.5% yeast with an 18-hour cold ferment is my sweet spot."

**Hydration depends on your flour.** Different flours absorb water differently. Whole wheat and rye absorb more water than white flour. Tipo 00 absorbs less than bread flour. If you switch flours, you may need to adjust hydration by 2–5% to get the same dough feel.

**Use percentages to troubleshoot.** If your bread is dense, check: is hydration too low? If it's bland, check: is salt under 1.8%? If it's over-proofing, check: is yeast too high for the fermentation time? Baker's percentages make diagnostic comparisons objective.

**Round to practical precision.** For home baking, rounding salt to the nearest gram and yeast to the nearest half-gram is fine. Don't stress about 0.3g differences — your kitchen scale probably isn't that accurate anyway.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is baker's percentage?

Baker's percentage is a formula system where every ingredient is expressed as a percentage of the total flour weight. Flour is always 100%, and all other ingredients are relative to flour. For example, "65% hydration" means the water weighs 65% of what the flour weighs. It's the standard notation used by professional bakers worldwide.

### Why don't baker's percentages add up to 100%?

Because baker's percentages use flour as the reference point, not the total recipe. Flour is always 100%, and every other ingredient adds to that total. A typical bread formula might total 165–200%+. This is by design — it makes scaling and comparing recipes much easier than traditional percentage systems.

### How do I calculate hydration?

Divide the total water weight by the total flour weight and multiply by 100. If your recipe uses 350g water and 500g flour, hydration = (350 ÷ 500) × 100 = 70%. Remember to include water from pre-ferments and starters in the total water if you want true hydration.

### What's a good hydration for bread?

It depends on the bread type and your experience level. For beginners, 60–65% hydration produces a manageable, firm dough. For most artisan breads, 68–72% is the sweet spot. For open-crumb sourdough, 75–80%. For ciabatta, 80–85%. Higher hydration requires more skill to handle but produces lighter, more open bread.

### How do I convert a recipe to baker's percentages?

Identify the total flour weight (add all flours together). Then divide each ingredient's weight by the total flour weight and multiply by 100. That's the baker's percentage for each ingredient. Flour will always be 100%.

### Can baker's percentages be used for pastry and cakes?

Yes, though they're less commonly used outside of bread and pizza baking. The system works for any recipe where flour is the primary structural ingredient. For cakes and pastries where sugar or butter might exceed the flour weight, the percentages will go above 100% for those ingredients (e.g., a butter cake might have 110% sugar, 100% butter) — the math still works perfectly.

### What does "inoculation percentage" mean in sourdough?

Inoculation percentage is the amount of sourdough starter (or levain) expressed as a baker's percentage of the flour weight. At 20% inoculation with 500g flour, you'd use 100g of starter. Higher inoculation means faster fermentation; lower inoculation means slower fermentation with more flavor development.
