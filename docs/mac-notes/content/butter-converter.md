## Understanding US Butter Stick Markings

If you've ever looked closely at a US butter wrapper, you'll notice tablespoon markings printed along the side. Each stick is marked from 1 to 8 tablespoons, making it easy to cut precise amounts without measuring.

Here's how to use those markings:

**Need 3 tablespoons?** Cut the stick at the "3" line. **Need ¼ cup?** That's 4 tablespoons — cut at the halfway point of the stick. **Need ⅓ cup?** That's 5⅓ tablespoons — cut between the "5" and "6" lines, slightly closer to "5."

The wrapper markings are surprisingly accurate on most brands. But if your butter is unwrapped (bought in bulk, or already softened), here's the tablespoon-to-stick conversion in practical terms:

| Amount Needed  | In Sticks           | How to Cut               |
|---------------|:-------------------:|--------------------------|
| 1 tablespoon  | ⅛ stick             | Cut at the "1" mark      |
| 2 tablespoons | ¼ stick             | Cut at the "2" mark      |
| 3 tablespoons | ⅜ stick             | Cut at the "3" mark      |
| ¼ cup (4 tbsp)| ½ stick             | Cut the stick in half    |
| ⅓ cup (5⅓ tbsp)| ⅔ stick           | Cut between "5" and "6"  |
| ½ cup (8 tbsp)| 1 full stick        | Use the whole stick      |
| ⅔ cup (10⅔ tbsp) | 1⅓ sticks       | 1 stick + cut at "2½"   |
| ¾ cup (12 tbsp)| 1½ sticks          | 1 stick + half stick     |
| 1 cup (16 tbsp)| 2 sticks           | Use 2 full sticks        |

## International Butter Sizes

Butter sticks are a uniquely American measurement. The rest of the world packages butter in blocks of various sizes, and if you're converting between a European, Australian, or American recipe, the differences matter.

### United States
- **Standard stick:** 113.4 g (4 oz / ½ cup / 8 tbsp)
- **Package:** 4 sticks = 1 pound (453.6 g)
- **Shape:** Long, narrow rectangle (about 4.75" × 1.25" × 1.25" / 12 × 3 × 3 cm)
- **West Coast variant:** Some western US brands (notably Tillamook, Challenge) sell "stubbier" sticks that are shorter and wider but contain the same 113.4 g

### Europe (EU Standard)
- **Standard block:** 250 g (8.82 oz)
- **Also available:** 125 g (4.41 oz), 500 g (17.64 oz)
- **Shape:** Wide, flat rectangle
- **Note:** 250 g is close to but NOT equal to 2 US sticks (226.8 g). The difference is 23.2 g — about 1.6 tablespoons. This matters in baking.

### United Kingdom
- **Standard block:** 250 g
- **Also available:** 500 g
- **Shape:** Similar to European standard
- **Note:** UK recipes sometimes call for butter in "knobs" — an informal measurement roughly equal to 1–2 tablespoons (15–30 g). There's no precise standard.

### Australia & New Zealand
- **Standard block:** 250 g
- **Also available:** 500 g
- **Shape:** Wide block, similar to European
- **Note:** Australian tablespoons are 20 ml (4 teaspoons), not 15 ml like US tablespoons. So "2 tablespoons of butter" in an Australian recipe is about 40 g, not 28 g.

### Japan
- **Standard block:** 200 g (7.05 oz)
- **Also available:** 150 g, 450 g
- **Note:** Butter is significantly more expensive in Japan, and recipes tend to use smaller quantities.

### Quick International Cross-Reference

| US Measurement   | European Equivalent  | Australian Equivalent |
|-----------------|:-------------------:|:---------------------:|
| 1 stick (113.4 g) | Just under ½ block | Just under ½ block   |
| 2 sticks (226.8 g) | Just under 1 block | Just under 1 block  |
| 1 US tbsp (14.2 g) | 14 g              | ¾ Aus tbsp           |
| ¼ cup (56.7 g)  | About ¼ block       | About ¼ block        |

## Converting Butter in Recipes: A Practical Guide

Most butter conversions are straightforward, but a few situations trip people up.

### Melted vs. Solid Butter

When a recipe says "½ cup melted butter," do you measure before or after melting? The answer: it doesn't matter much, because butter is nearly incompressible. A stick of butter occupies the same volume whether it's solid or melted. The tiny difference (less than 1%) comes from air incorporated into softened butter, which escapes when melted.

However, **"½ cup butter, melted"** and **"½ cup melted butter"** technically mean different things. The first means measure solid butter (1 stick), then melt it. The second means melt butter first, then measure ½ cup of liquid. In practice, most home cooks (and most recipe writers) use these interchangeably.

### Softened Butter

"Softened butter" means butter at about 65–68°F (18–20°C) — it yields slightly when pressed but holds its shape. This is critical for creaming butter and sugar. Cold butter straight from the fridge is about 35°F (2°C). Leave it on the counter for 30–60 minutes, or cut it into small pieces to speed up the process. Avoid microwaving — you'll get unevenly softened butter with melted pockets.

### Substituting Oil for Butter

A common conversion question: if a recipe calls for butter and you want to use oil instead, use about 75–80% of the butter amount. So 1 cup butter (2 sticks / 226.8 g) becomes ¾ cup oil (about 170–180 ml). This works because butter is only about 80% fat — the rest is water and milk solids. Oil is 100% fat.

This substitution works well for muffins, quick breads, and cakes. It doesn't work for flaky pastry, pie crusts, or anything that depends on solid butter creating layers.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: European Recipe Calls for 200 g Butter

An Italian cake recipe calls for 200 g of butter. How many sticks is that? 200 ÷ 113.4 = 1.76 sticks. Use 1¾ sticks (cut the second stick at the "6" mark, giving you 6 tablespoons = 85 g, plus the full first stick = 113.4 g, totaling 198.4 g). Close enough for any recipe.

### Example 2: How Many Cups in a Stick of Butter?

One stick of butter is exactly ½ cup. Two sticks = 1 cup. If a recipe calls for ¾ cup of butter, that's 1½ sticks — one full stick plus half of another stick (cut at the "4" line).

### Example 3: Scaling a Cookie Recipe

A cookie recipe calls for 10 tablespoons of butter. You have sticks. 10 tablespoons ÷ 8 tablespoons per stick = 1.25 sticks. Use one full stick and cut a second stick at the "2" mark.

### Example 4: Converting an Australian Recipe

An Australian brownie recipe calls for 125 g butter and 2 tablespoons melted butter. In US terms: 125 g ≈ 1 stick plus 1 tablespoon. But remember, Australian tablespoons are 20 ml. So "2 tablespoons" in the recipe = 40 ml ≈ 2.7 US tablespoons, or about 38 g. Total butter needed: about 163 g, or just under 1½ US sticks.

### Example 5: Clarified Butter / Ghee Conversion

A recipe calls for ½ cup clarified butter (ghee). Clarifying removes the water and milk solids, so you lose about 20% of the original butter. To get ½ cup (113 g) of clarified butter, start with about ⅔ cup (150 g) of regular butter — roughly 1⅓ sticks.

### Example 6: Replacing Butter with a European Block

Your pie crust recipe calls for 2½ sticks of cold butter (283.5 g). You bought European 250 g blocks. One full block plus about 2⅓ tablespoons (33.5 g) from a second block will give you the right amount. Or just use one block and accept the pie crust will be slightly less rich — 250 g vs. 283.5 g is a 12% difference that most people won't notice in a savory pie.

## Salted vs. Unsalted Butter in Conversions

When converting butter amounts, the salted/unsalted distinction doesn't affect volume or weight — a stick of salted butter and a stick of unsalted butter weigh the same 113.4 grams.

The difference is in salt content. Salted butter typically contains about 1.5–1.7% salt by weight, or roughly ¼ teaspoon of salt per stick (about 90 mg sodium per tablespoon). If a recipe calls for unsalted butter and you're using salted, reduce any added salt in the recipe by ¼ teaspoon per stick of butter.

Most bakers prefer unsalted butter for two reasons: it gives you full control over the salt level, and unsalted butter tends to be fresher (salt acts as a preservative, meaning salted butter can sit on shelves longer).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many cups in a stick of butter?
One standard US stick of butter equals exactly ½ cup (8 tablespoons, 4 ounces, 113.4 grams). Two sticks equal 1 cup, and four sticks equal 2 cups (1 pound).

### How many tablespoons in a stick of butter?
There are 8 tablespoons in one US stick of butter. Each tablespoon is about 14.2 grams. The markings on the butter wrapper show each tablespoon increment from 1 to 8.

### How much is half a stick of butter?
Half a stick of butter is ¼ cup, 4 tablespoons, 2 ounces, or 56.7 grams. Cut the stick at the "4" tablespoon marking on the wrapper.

### How much is 1 stick of butter in grams?
One US stick of butter is 113.4 grams. This is slightly less than half a European 250 g block (which is 125 g cut in half). For most recipes, 113 g or 115 g is close enough when measuring by weight.

### Is a stick of butter the same worldwide?
No. Butter sticks are a US-only measurement. Europe, Australia, and most other countries sell butter in 250 g or 500 g blocks. Even within the US, west coast brands sometimes package sticks in a shorter, wider shape, though the weight remains 113.4 g.

### How do I measure butter without a scale?
Use the wrapper markings. Each US stick is marked with 8 tablespoon lines and ¼ cup intervals. If you need 3 tablespoons, cut at the "3" line. For unwrapped butter, the water displacement method works: fill a measuring cup with water to the 1-cup line, then add butter until the water reaches the amount you need. For ¼ cup butter, add butter until the water rises from 1 cup to 1¼ cups.

### Can I substitute margarine 1:1 for butter?
In most recipes, yes — margarine (stick form, not tub) can be substituted 1:1 for butter by volume and weight. However, margarine has slightly higher water content and lower fat, which can affect texture in pastry and cookies. Tub margarine has even higher water content and shouldn't be used in baking at all.

### How long does butter last?
Unsalted butter keeps for about 1 month in the refrigerator and 6–9 months in the freezer. Salted butter lasts about 2 months refrigerated. Butter freezes well with no texture change — wrap tightly in foil or freezer bags to prevent freezer burn and flavor absorption.
