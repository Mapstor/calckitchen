## How the Pizza Dough Calculator Works

Every pizza dough is defined by a set of **baker's percentages** — a system where every ingredient is expressed as a percentage of the flour weight (flour is always 100%). Different pizza styles use different percentages for hydration (water), salt, yeast, and oil, which is what gives each style its distinctive texture and character.

The calculator uses this formula:

**Total dough weight = Number of pizzas × Ball weight per pizza**

Then it works backward from the total dough weight using the baker's percentages to calculate how much flour, water, salt, yeast, oil, and sugar you need.

The formula for flour weight is:

**Flour = Total dough weight ÷ (1 + hydration% + salt% + yeast% + oil% + sugar%)**

Once you have the flour weight, every other ingredient is simply: **Ingredient weight = Flour × (ingredient percentage ÷ 100)**.

## Pizza Style Presets with Exact Baker's Percentages

### Neapolitan (AVPN Standard)

The Associazione Verace Pizza Napoletana (AVPN) sets the official standard for authentic Neapolitan pizza. This is a purist's dough — just flour, water, salt, and yeast. No oil, no sugar.

| Ingredient | Baker's % | For 4 Pizzas (250g balls) |
|---|---|---|
| Flour (Tipo 00) | 100% | 611g |
| Water | 60% | 366g |
| Salt | 2.8% | 17g |
| Yeast (fresh) | 0.2% | 1.2g |
| Oil | 0% | 0g |
| Sugar | 0% | 0g |
| **Total dough** | **163%** | **~1000g** |

**Hydration range:** 55–65% (60% is the standard starting point; increase to 62–65% as your handling skills improve)

**Ball weight:** 200–280g (250g is standard for a 10–12" pizza)

**Bake:** 900°F (480°C) for 60–90 seconds in a wood-fired or high-temperature pizza oven

**Flour note:** Use Tipo 00 flour (Caputo Pizzeria is the gold standard). Tipo 00 is finely milled with moderate protein (12–12.5%), which produces the characteristic soft, tender, slightly charred crust with a puffy cornicione (raised edge). If you can't find 00 flour, King Arthur bread flour is a reasonable substitute, though the texture will be slightly different.

**Yeast note:** AVPN specifies fresh (compressed) yeast. If using active dry yeast, use 40% of the fresh amount (0.08%). If using instant/rapid-rise yeast, use 33% (0.07%). The very low yeast amount is intentional — Neapolitan dough relies on a long, slow fermentation.

New York pizza is defined by its large, foldable slices with a crispy-yet-chewy crust. The addition of oil and sugar gives it a richer flavor and promotes browning in a standard home oven.

**Bake:** 500–550°F (260–290°C) for 12–15 minutes on a pizza stone or steel

**Flour note:** Bread flour with 12–14% protein content works best. The higher protein produces more gluten, giving NY pizza its characteristic chewy stretch. All-purpose flour works but yields a softer, less chewy crust.

## Fermentation Options

Fermentation is what develops flavor in pizza dough. Longer, slower fermentation creates more complex flavors and better texture. The calculator supports three pre-ferment options.

### Direct Method (Same-Day)

The simplest approach. Mix all ingredients, bulk ferment at room temperature for 1–2 hours, divide into balls, and proof for 4–8 hours (or 24–72 hours in the fridge for better flavor).

**Yeast amounts in the presets above are calibrated for direct method with a same-day bake or 24-hour cold ferment.**

### Poolish (Polish Pre-Ferment)

A poolish is a wet pre-ferment made with equal parts flour and water plus a small amount of yeast, mixed 12–18 hours before making the final dough. It adds extensibility, flavor complexity, and a slightly more open crumb.

**How to incorporate poolish:**
- Take 20–30% of the total flour and equal weight of water from the recipe
- Add a tiny amount of yeast (0.1% of poolish flour weight)
- Mix, cover, and ferment 12–18 hours at room temperature until bubbly and domed
- Add to remaining ingredients when making the final dough

**Poolish Example for 4 NY-style pizzas (730g total flour):**

| Poolish Component | Amount |
|---|---|
| Flour (from recipe total) | 220g (30% of 730g) |
| Water (from recipe total) | 220g |
| Yeast (instant) | 0.2g (pinch) |
| Ferment time | 12–18 hours at room temp |

Final dough uses remaining 510g flour, 240g water, and all salt/oil/sugar.

### Biga (Italian Pre-Ferment)

A biga is a stiffer pre-ferment (50–60% hydration) that produces a stronger gluten network and a more pronounced wheat flavor. It's traditional for Italian bread and pizza doughs.

**How to incorporate biga:**
- Take 20–30% of total flour with 50% of that flour weight in water
- Add a pinch of yeast
- Mix into a stiff ball, cover, ferment 16–24 hours at cool room temp (65°F / 18°C ideal)
- Break into pieces and incorporate into final dough

**Biga Example for 4 NY-style pizzas:**

| Biga Component | Amount |
|---|---|
| Flour (from recipe total) | 220g |
| Water (from recipe total) | 110g (50% of biga flour) |
| Yeast (instant) | 0.2g |
| Ferment time | 16–24 hours at 60–68°F (16–20°C) |

### Cold Ferment (Retarding)

Cold fermentation means shaping your dough balls and placing them in the refrigerator for an extended period. This slows yeast activity, allowing enzymes to break down starches and develop more complex flavors.

| Cold Ferment Duration | Yeast Adjustment | Flavor Impact |
|---|---|---|
| 24 hours | Use recipe amount | Mild improvement over same-day |
| 48 hours | Reduce yeast by 25% | Noticeable flavor complexity |
| 72 hours | Reduce yeast by 40–50% | Deep, complex flavor. Sweet spot for most styles. |
| 96+ hours | Reduce yeast by 50–60% | Very complex but risk of over-fermentation |

For home bakers, a **72-hour cold ferment** is widely considered the sweet spot — dramatic flavor improvement without the risk of the dough over-proofing. Remove dough balls from the fridge 2 hours before baking to allow them to come to room temperature and relax for easy stretching.

## Understanding Hydration

Hydration is the single most important variable in pizza dough. It's the percentage of water relative to flour weight.

**Lower hydration (55–60%):** Easier to handle, shape, and stretch. Produces a denser, crisper crust. Best for beginners and for Neapolitan style.

**Medium hydration (60–65%):** The sweet spot for most home bakers. Manageable to work with but produces a better rise and more tender crumb than low hydration.

**High hydration (70–75%):** Wet, sticky, and challenging to handle. Produces an open, airy, hole-filled crumb. Required for Detroit and Sicilian styles. Use wet hands and bench scrapers instead of trying to shape by hand.

**For beginners:** Start at 58–60% hydration and increase by 2% each time you bake until you find your comfort level. High hydration produces better pizza, but only if you can handle the dough without degassing it.

## Pro Tips for Perfect Pizza Dough

**Weigh everything.** Volume measurements (cups) are wildly inaccurate for flour — a cup of flour can weigh anywhere from 120g to 160g depending on how you scoop it. Use a kitchen scale accurate to 1 gram. This is the single biggest improvement most home pizza makers can make.

**Water temperature matters.** In winter, use warm water (80–85°F / 27–29°C) to give the yeast a head start. In summer, use cold water (55–65°F / 13–18°C) to slow fermentation and prevent over-proofing. Aim for a final dough temperature of 75–78°F (24–26°C).

**Don't over-knead.** Pizza dough needs enough kneading to develop gluten (8–12 minutes by hand, 5–7 in a mixer), but over-kneading makes the dough tight and hard to stretch. The dough is ready when it passes the windowpane test — stretch a small piece thin enough to see light through it without tearing.

**Use your fridge.** Cold fermentation is the easiest way to dramatically improve your pizza. Even 24 hours in the fridge makes a noticeable difference in flavor and texture compared to same-day dough.

**Oil your containers, not your dough.** When placing dough balls in containers for proofing, oil the container rather than the dough surface. This prevents sticking while keeping the dough surface tacky enough to hold toppings and char properly.

**Don't skip the bench rest.** After dividing and shaping dough balls, let them rest at room temperature for at least 1–2 hours before stretching (longer if cold from the fridge). Cold, tight dough tears instead of stretching.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What hydration should I use for pizza dough?

Start at 60% hydration if you're a beginner. This produces a manageable dough that's easy to stretch and shape. As you gain experience, gradually increase to 63–65% for better texture and crumb. Detroit and Sicilian styles require 70–75% hydration for their characteristic airy structure.

### How much dough do I need per pizza?

It depends on the style and size. For Neapolitan (10–12" pizza): 200–280g per ball. For New York (14–16"): 280–350g. For Detroit (10×14" pan): 450–550g. For Sicilian (sheet pan): 500–700g.

### Can I use all-purpose flour instead of bread flour?

Yes, but the results will be different. All-purpose flour has less protein (10–11%) than bread flour (12–14%), so the dough will be softer and less chewy. For New York and Detroit styles, bread flour makes a noticeable difference. For Neapolitan, Tipo 00 is preferred, but all-purpose is closer to 00 than bread flour is.

### How long should I ferment pizza dough?

Minimum: 4–8 hours at room temperature for acceptable results. Better: 24 hours cold ferment in the fridge. Best: 48–72 hours cold ferment for maximum flavor development. Reduce yeast by 25–50% for ferments longer than 24 hours.

### What's the difference between fresh yeast and dry yeast?

Fresh (compressed) yeast is sold in blocks and has a mild flavor preferred by traditional pizzaiolos. To convert: Active dry yeast = 40% of fresh yeast weight. Instant yeast = 33% of fresh yeast weight. So if a recipe calls for 3g fresh yeast, use 1.2g active dry or 1g instant.

### Do I need a pizza oven for Neapolitan pizza?

For authentic Neapolitan with 60–90 second bake times, yes — you need an oven that reaches 800–900°F (like an Ooni, Gozney, or Breville Pizzaiolo). A regular home oven maxes out at 500–550°F, which works great for New York, Detroit, and Sicilian styles but can't replicate the rapid char of true Neapolitan pizza.

### What's a poolish and is it worth the effort?

A poolish is a wet pre-ferment (equal parts flour and water with a tiny amount of yeast) that you mix 12–18 hours before making your dough. It adds flavor complexity, improves crumb structure, and makes the dough more extensible. It's absolutely worth the effort — the only "cost" is planning ahead by one night.
